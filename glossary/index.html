Glossary

Definitions of the terms used in this course, written as we use them rather than as a dictionary would. Where a term is best understood against its opposite number, the entry says so — stress against strain, joint against vein, cleavage against schistosity.

A useful general glossary of geological terms is at opengeology.org.

Allochthon

A nappe or tectonic unit transported far from where it formed, now sitting on a detachment above rocks with an entirely different history. Establishing which units are allochthonous is one of the first things to do in a thrust terrane, because it tells you which contacts are tectonic rather than stratigraphic.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust Faults and Tectonic Units

Anderson’s theory of faulting

Anderson’s (1905) argument that because the Earth’s surface is a free surface carrying no shear stress, one principal stress must be vertical and the other two horizontal — which allows exactly three tectonic regimes. Vertical σ1\sigma_1σ1​ gives normal faulting, vertical σ2\sigma_2σ2​ strike-slip, vertical σ3\sigma_3σ3​ thrusting. It assumes coaxial deformation and isotropic rock, so treat it as a first-order guide rather than a law.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Anderson’s Theory of Faulting · 3.1 Stress — Anderson’s Theory of Faulting (1905)

Angular velocity (of a plate)

The rate at which a plate rotates about its Euler pole, usually in degrees per million years. With the pole position it completely describes a rigid plate’s motion, and the surface velocity anywhere is the angular velocity times the perpendicular distance to the rotation axis.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Angular Strain · 1.1 Global Deformation Patterns — Global Plate Motions

Anticline

A fold that closes upwards and has the oldest rocks in its core. Note the distinction from an antiform: “anticline” is a statement about the stratigraphy, so you can only use it if you know the way-up of the beds.

Slides: 5.1 Fold Geometry — Anticline

Anticlinorium

A large composite anticline — a regional upward-closing structure whose limbs and hinge carry many smaller folds. Its downward-closing counterpart is a synclinorium.

Antiform

A fold that closes upwards, described purely by geometry. Use this when the way-up of the beds is unknown or when they are overturned, and reserve “anticline” for cases where you know the core is oldest.

Asthenosphere

A rheological domain: the weaker zone beneath the plates that allows them to move freely. It is defined mechanically rather than chemically, so its boundary with the lithosphere is a strength contrast, not a change of composition.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — Datasets: lithospheric thickness (LAB)

Autochthon

Rock that has not been tectonically transported — the in-situ basement over which an allochthon has been thrust. Units that have moved only a short distance are parautochthonous, a useful hedge for rocks clearly disturbed but not far-travelled.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust Faults and Tectonic Units

Auxiliary plane

The second nodal plane of a focal mechanism, perpendicular to the fault plane, producing exactly the same radiation pattern. This is the origin of the classic focal-mechanism ambiguity: seismology alone cannot tell you which plane actually slipped, so you need the geology or the aftershock distribution to decide.

Axial planar cleavage

Cleavage running roughly parallel to the axial surface of the folds it accompanies, because both record the same shortening. It is invaluable in mapping: it keeps a consistent orientation across a whole fold train, so the angle between bedding and cleavage tells you where you sit relative to the nearest hinge and which way the beds young.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Axial planar cleavage

Axial surface

The surface containing the hinge lines of all the folded layers in a fold. Often called the axial plane, but it need not be planar — in many real folds it is curved.

Slides: 5.1 Fold Geometry — Basic Elements of Fold Morphology

Axial trace

The line where the axial surface of a fold meets the ground surface, or any other section through the fold. It is what you actually draw on a map.

Back-arc basin

An extensional basin opening behind a volcanic arc, on the overriding plate, generally driven by slab rollback pulling the upper plate apart.

Basal drag

The shear traction exerted on the base of a plate by the convecting mantle beneath. Depending on whether the mantle moves faster or slower than the plate, it can either drive or resist the motion; Couette flow is the simple model for it.

Basin (fold)

A fold closing downwards in all directions — a doubly plunging synform, the structural opposite of a dome. Not to be confused with a sedimentary basin, which is a region of subsidence rather than a fold.

Slides: 5.1 Fold Geometry — Basin

Bending (forced folding)

Folding caused by forces applied across the layering rather than along it — over a reactivated basement fault, above a thrust ramp, over a rising diapir, or between separating boudins. Because they are side effects of some larger structure forcing the layers to bend, bending folds have no characteristic wavelength.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Bending · 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Plate Bending / Unbending Stresses

Beta factor

The stretching factor of a rifted region, β=l/l0\beta = l/l_0β=l/l0​, so β=2\beta = 2β=2 means the crust has been stretched to twice its width and, to first order, thinned to half its thickness. Beta is the standard currency for comparing rifts and for predicting a basin’s subsidence and heat-flow history.

Blind thrust

A thrust that dies out before reaching the surface, so it has no surface trace and its slip is taken up instead by folding of the rocks above. They are hard to map, they host earthquakes, and the folds above them trap hydrocarbons.

Body force

A force acting throughout the whole volume of a material — in geology, essentially gravity. Contrast a surface force, which acts across a surface: stress is a surface force divided by the area it acts on, and body forces are what generate those stresses in the first place.

Borehole breakout

A method of measuring in-situ stress: the wall of a drillhole spalls off preferentially in the direction of the minimum horizontal stress, making the hole elliptical. Imaging the hole shape gives the orientation of the horizontal stresses; modelling it gives their magnitudes.

Boudinage

The break-up of a competent layer stretched within a weaker matrix into a train of sausage-shaped segments (French boudin). Boudins record layer-parallel extension, their necks localise veining and fluid flow, and rotated asymmetric boudins are a reliable sense-of-shear indicator.

Buckling (active folding)

Folding driven by the layer itself — a competent layer in a weaker matrix, shortened along its own length, becomes unstable and amplifies small irregularities into folds. It needs layer-parallel shortening, a viscosity contrast and initial irregularities; the dominant wavelength depends on layer thickness and viscosity contrast, which is why thick stiff layers make first-order folds and thin ones make parasitic folds.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Active Folding or Buckling

Bulk modulus (KKK)

The elastic stiffness against uniform compression — how much pressure it takes to produce a given fractional change in volume. It is the elastic constant paired with the mean (pressure) part of the stress tensor, as the shear modulus is paired with the deviatoric part.

Byerlee’s law

The experimental result that the friction of nearly all rock types is much the same, so the shear stress needed to slide a fault is roughly 0.6 times the normal stress clamping it shut (τ≈0.6 σn\tau \approx 0.6\,\sigma_nτ≈0.6σn​). At 5 km depth that is about 80 MPa of shear stress on a 130 MPa normal stress; since earthquake stress drops are only 1–10 MPa, faults sit permanently close to failure and small perturbations can tip them.

Cataclasite

A cohesive fault rock formed by cataclastic flow — grain fracturing, rotation, crushing and localised shear banding — at the higher pressures and temperatures of roughly 4–8 km depth. It differs from breccia and gouge in being cohesive, because comminution has produced a fine interlocking matrix.

Cleavage

A foliation along which the rock preferentially splits — a subset of foliation, since not every foliated rock cleaves. It is the low-temperature end of the tectonic foliation spectrum, developing below about 350 °C and best in phyllosilicate-rich rocks. Do not confuse it with crystallographic cleavage, which is a single mineral breaking on lattice planes.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Cleavage refraction

Coaxial deformation

Deformation in which the same material lines remain the principal strain axes throughout, so the strain ellipsoid keeps its orientation and only changes shape. Pure shear is the type example, and Anderson’s theory of faulting quietly assumes it. Contrast non-coaxial deformation.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Volume Change

Coefficient of friction (μ\muμ)

The proportionality between the shear stress needed to slide a surface and the normal stress clamping it shut, about 0.6 for typical rocks. It sets the angle at which faults form through tan⁡2θ=∓1/μ\tan 2\theta = \mp 1/\mutan2θ=∓1/μ, giving roughly 30° between the fault and σ1\sigma_1σ1​ — which is why fresh normal faults dip near 60° and thrusts near 30°.

Cohesive strength (CCC)

The shear stress a rock can carry across a plane even with no normal stress squeezing it — the intercept of the Coulomb failure line. Intact rock has real cohesion; a pre-existing fault surface has almost none, which is why old faults reactivate before new ones form.

Competence

The relative resistance of a rock layer to deformation compared with its neighbours — a comparative notion rather than an absolute material property. Competent (stiff) layers such as sandstone or dolomite tend to be cut by ramps and to buckle; incompetent (weak) layers such as shale, clay and salt flow, take up flats and décollements, and localise detachments.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — 3 fundamental mechanisms

Competence contrast

The difference in effective strength or viscosity between adjacent layers — the single most important control on how a layered rock deforms. High contrast gives buckling, boudinage and long-wavelength folds; as temperature rises the contrast falls and passive folding takes over.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — 3 fundamental mechanisms

Confining pressure

The all-round squeezing a rock experiences at depth, which is what stops it falling apart; in the laboratory it is the pressure in the vessel, in the Earth essentially the lithostatic pressure. Raising it makes fractures harder to open, so rock becomes stronger and sustains larger strains before failing — the main reason strength initially increases downwards.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Effect of Confining Pressure · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Confining Pressure and Temperature

Contractional structure

Any structure that accommodates shortening, whether by faulting, folding or volume loss. Note the vocabulary trap: “contractional” describes what the structure achieves, not its type, so a normal fault can be a contractional structure if it accommodates layer-parallel shortening of a tilted bed.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Contractional faults · 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Transfer faults in contractional regime

Couette flow

The textbook viscous flow between two surfaces, one of which moves at a fixed velocity, giving a linear velocity profile and a uniform strain rate. It is the simplest model for basal drag — mantle sheared beneath a moving plate — and gives you the shear stress on the plate base for free.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Couette Flow (advanced)

Coulomb–Mohr failure criterion

The rule that intact rock fractures in shear when τ=C+μ σn\tau = C + \mu\,\sigma_nτ=C+μσn​, with CCC the cohesive strength and μ\muμ the coefficient of internal friction (typically 0.47–0.7). On a Mohr diagram it is a straight line, and the rock fails when the circle touches it. Its geological payoff is Anderson’s theory of faulting: conjugate shears at 20–30° to σ1\sigma_1σ1​.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — The Coulomb-mohr Failure Criterion · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — The Coulomb-mohr Failure Criteria

Craton

A region of the Earth’s crust that has experienced little or no tectonic deformation for at least a billion years. Australia’s cratons (Yilgarn, Pilbara, Gawler) carry unusually thick lithosphere, over 200 km, and crust exceeding 50 km.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — The names of things

Creep

Slow, continuous, permanent deformation under a stress well below the short-term breaking strength. It is the umbrella term for the crystal-scale processes — diffusion, dislocation motion, pressure solution — that let solid rock flow, and it is what viscous and power-law rheologies describe at the macroscopic scale.

Crenulation cleavage

A later cleavage formed by microfolding an existing foliation into trains of parallel microfolds less than a centimetre across. It is direct evidence of a second deformation phase, and whether the crenulations are symmetric or asymmetric depends on the angle between the old fabric and the new shortening direction.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Secondary tectonic cleavage · 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Geometric lineations

Crest line

The line joining the topographically highest points on a folded surface. It coincides with the hinge line only in an upright fold — in an inclined fold the two are distinct, which is a classic source of confusion.

Critical taper

The idea that an orogenic or accretionary wedge self-organises to a surface slope and basal dip at which it is everywhere on the verge of failing internally, set by the balance between friction on the basal detachment and the strength of the wedge itself, modified by erosion. High basal friction or a weak wedge forces deformation back into the interior; a strong wedge on a slippery base gives a long, thin, gently tapered belt.

Culmination

A high point along the hinge line of a fold, where the fold axis reaches a local maximum in elevation and plunges away in both directions. Its low-point counterpart is a depression.

Cylindrical fold

A fold whose surface can be generated by moving a straight line (the fold axis) parallel to itself, so any section perpendicular to that axis has the same shape. Real folds are only cylindrical over limited domains, which is exactly why we break a map area into structural domains before plotting stereonets.

Damage zone

The volume of wall rock either side of a fault core where the density of brittle structures — subsidiary fractures, deformation bands, small faults, stylolites — is raised above the regional background. It accommodates only a small share of the slip but is far wider than the core, and it usually controls the fault’s bulk permeability.

Slides: 4.4 The Tohoku Earthquake — Financial Cost of Damage

Décollement

A weak, sub-horizontal surface or thin layer, often shale, clay or salt, on which the overlying rock package detaches and slides independently of what lies beneath. It is the basal thrust that the imbricate faults of a foreland thrust belt flatten into, and the floor along which a whole orogenic wedge is dragged forward.

Deformation

Any process that changes the shape, size or position of a body. It splits into rigid-body components — translation and rotation — and non-rigid components, distortion (change of shape) and dilation (change of volume). Only the non-rigid part is strain.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Deformation · 3.3 Rheology — Deformation Analog · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Marble Deformation

Deformation mechanism map

A plot of temperature against differential stress (or grain size) showing which deformation mechanism dominates in each region of parameter space. It makes the depth story visual: fracture and frictional sliding in the cool, shallow lithosphere, creep in the hot interior.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Deformation · 3.3 Rheology — Deformation Analog · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Marble Deformation

Depression

A low point along the hinge line of a fold, where the fold axis reaches a local minimum and plunges towards it from both directions. The counterpart of a culmination.

Detachment

The low-angle fault or shear zone separating transported rock from rock that has stayed put. Used almost interchangeably with décollement in contractional settings, but the term also covers the low-angle normal faults that unroof metamorphic core complexes — so always check the sense of slip.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust Faults and Tectonic Units

Deviatoric stress

What is left of the stress tensor once the mean (pressure) part is subtracted — the anisotropic, orientation-dependent part. It is the deviatoric stress that actually distorts and breaks rocks; pressure alone just squeezes uniformly.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Deviatoric Stress and Mean Stress

Differential stress (σ1−σ3\sigma_1 - \sigma_3σ1​−σ3​)

The difference between the largest and smallest principal stresses — the diameter of the Mohr circle, and a direct measure of how far a rock is from being uniformly pressurised. It is the quantity that drives fracturing and flow, and the horizontal axis of most strength and deformation-map plots.

Diffuse plate boundary

A broad zone of distributed deformation, tens to thousands of kilometres wide, taking up relative motion instead of a single narrow fault. The Indo-Australian plate is internally divided by exactly such zones — a useful early warning that “rigid plate” is an idealisation.

Diffusion creep

Plastic flow by the migration of atoms and vacancies through or around grains, favoured by high temperature, low stress and small grain size. It contrasts with dislocation creep in being grain-size sensitive and in producing little crystallographic preferred orientation.

Dilation (volumetric strain)

The fractional change in volume of a rock. Positive dilation means expansion (veining, fracturing); negative means volume loss, most commonly by compaction and pressure solution as porosity is squeezed out with burial.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Dilation / Compaction zones · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Deformation

Dislocation creep

Plastic flow by the movement of line defects through crystal lattices as atomic bonds are successively rearranged, with no microfracturing. It dominates at moderate to high temperature and higher stress, produces strong crystallographic preferred orientations — which is why quartz c-axis fabrics record shear sense — and underlies most mylonitic microstructures.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — What Deformation Mechanisms are plastic?

Displacement vector

The arrow joining a particle’s initial position to its final position. The whole deformation is encoded in the field of displacement vectors, and strain is obtained by differentiating that field in space.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Displacement Vector · 5.5 Shear Zones — Displacement in simple shear zones

Dome

A fold closing upwards in all directions — a doubly plunging antiform, the structural opposite of a basin.

Slides: 5.1 Fold Geometry — Dome

Domino (bookshelf) faulting

An array of parallel normal faults bounding blocks that all rotate together in the same sense, like books tilting on a shelf. In the ideal model the blocks do not deform internally and the throw is the same on every fault; real examples show substantial internal strain, so the “soft domino” version is usually more realistic.

Slides: 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Summary · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Geological Example of Rotation

Ductile deformation

Deformation without loss of cohesion, in which rock changes shape smoothly and retains intricate geometries when the forces stop — folds rather than fractures. It is favoured by high temperature, high confining pressure, the presence of fluids and slow strain rates.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Ductile shear zones · 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — The Brittle-Ductile Transition · 3.3 Rheology — Where we left off (Module 1.3)

Ductile shear zone

A shear zone in which passive markers can be traced continuously through the zone with no visible discontinuities at the scale of observation. Note the careful distinction: “ductile” describes the style of deformation and is scale-dependent, whereas “plastic” describes the mechanism — so a ductile zone can still work by brittle mechanisms at the grain scale.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Ductile shear zones · 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — The Brittle-Ductile Transition · 3.3 Rheology — Where we left off (Module 1.3)

Duplex

A stack of horses confined between an overlying roof thrust and an underlying floor thrust, formed when a propagating flat thrust sticks and successive ramps cut up in front of it. Duplexes are one of the main ways a thrust belt thickens itself without any single fault taking up all the shortening.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust systems geometries · 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Normal Fault systems

Dynamic recrystallisation

Recrystallisation occurring during deformation, in which strained grains are progressively replaced by new, smaller, strain-free grains as dislocations sweep through and grain boundaries migrate. It keeps mylonites fine-grained, and it is why an oblique grain-shape foliation records only the most recent strain increment while the main foliation integrates the whole history.

Dynamics

The analysis of the forces and stresses that caused a deformation. It is the hardest of the three analyses (with geometric and kinematic) because we have very limited access to stress in the Earth, present-day or past — hence the reliance on focal mechanisms, boreholes and modelling.

Effective stress (σ^\hat\sigmaσ^)

The stress actually carried by the rock’s grain framework, σ^=σn−pf\hat\sigma = \sigma_n - p_fσ^=σn​−pf​, because pore fluid pushing outwards supports part of the load. On a Mohr diagram, raising the fluid pressure slides the whole circle left towards the failure envelope without changing its size — a very cheap way to make a rock fail, and the mechanism behind hydraulic fracturing and injection-induced earthquakes.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Two terms: they may fight

Elastic deformation

Recoverable deformation — remove the stress and the material springs straight back, essentially instantaneously. Because nothing is left behind, elastic strain is invisible in the rock record, but it is how rocks store the energy that earthquakes release, and it dominates the cold, shallow crust.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Elastic Deformation · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Elastic Strain Caused by a Seismic Wave · 2.1 Structural Geology — Methods of Tectonics and Structural Geology

Elastic rebound

The earthquake half of the seismic cycle: between events, plate motion elastically strains the crust around a locked fault; in the earthquake the two sides snap back towards their unstrained shape, giving the coseismic displacement. Postseismic relaxation then continues for weeks to years.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Elastic Tensor · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Elastic Strain Caused by a Seismic Wave · 2.1 Structural Geology — Methods of Tectonics and Structural Geology

Elastica

The shape a thin elastic layer takes when it buckles far beyond the small-deflection limit — the exact large-deflection solution for a bent elastic beam. In structural geology it names the extreme fold form with tightly hooked, mushroom-shaped hinges and limbs that curve back past parallel.

Elongation (ε\varepsilonε, eee)

The fractional change in length of a line, ε=(l−l0)/l0\varepsilon = (l - l_0)/l_0ε=(l−l0​)/l0​ — positive for stretching, negative for shortening, and dimensionless. It is the simplest measure of strain and the one to fall back on when the tensor gets confusing.

Slides: 2.3 Extensional Regimes — General definition · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Longitudinal Strain

Euler pole (pole of rotation)

Because plates move on a sphere, the motion of a rigid plate is a rotation about an axis through the Earth’s centre, and the Euler pole is where that axis pierces the surface. This is why one rigid plate has different surface speeds in different places: speed grows with angular distance from the pole and is zero at the pole itself.

Fabric

The penetrative, repeated arrangement of grains and grain aggregates that pervades a rock at the microscopic to centimetre scale — elements more than about 10 cm apart, like faults, do not count. Primary fabrics are acquired when the rock forms (bedding, magmatic layering); secondary or tectonic fabrics form during deformation, and rocks with strong tectonic fabrics are tectonites.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Definition: Fabric · 3.3 Rheology — Effects of Foliation and Crystal Fabric · 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Tectonic lineations

Facing direction

The direction along the axial surface, perpendicular to the hinge line, in which the beds young. Facing is what turns a geometric description (antiform, synform) into a stratigraphic one (anticline, syncline), so establishing it is essential in polydeformed terranes.

Failure envelope

The curve on a Mohr diagram separating stable stress states, where the circle is clear of the curve, from unstable ones where it touches. The realistic composite envelope is parabolic in the tensile field (Griffith), straightens into the Coulomb line at moderate confining pressure, and flattens towards a horizontal yield line at high pressure — that flattening is effectively the brittle–plastic transition.

Slides: 4.1 Brittle Deformation — The Mohr Failure Envelope · 3.1 Stress — The Coulomb Failure Function

Fault

A discontinuity across which there is measurable shear displacement, appearing as an abrupt step in the displacement field. The boundary with “shear fracture” is one of scale and convention; and note that a fault is idealised as a surface, whereas any real structure with significant displacement is a fault zone with thickness.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Fault Zone · 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Normal Fault systems · 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — Will the fault slip ?

Fault breccia

An incohesive fault rock made of angular fragments larger than about 1 mm. Brecciation involves dilation and volume increase, so breccia points to low confining pressure or high fluid pressure — the shallow part of a fault zone.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Fault Breccia

Fault core

The narrow central part of a fault zone that takes up most of the displacement, where the protolith has been converted into fault rocks. Core thickness scales with displacement, statistically about 1/100 of the total slip for faults up to around 100 m of offset.

Fault gouge

The very fine-grained, often clay-rich and foliated fault rock produced by intense comminution in the principal slip zone. Where the gouge contains clay minerals absent from the protolith it records fluid–rock reaction inside the core, and clay-rich gouge is what makes some faults excellent seals.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Fault Gouge

Fault zone

A tabular volume containing many closely spaced, subparallel and often anastomosing slip surfaces enclosing lenses of broken wall rock, from centimetres to about a kilometre wide. Contrast “fault”, the idealised single surface: any fault with appreciable displacement is really a zone.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Fault Zone

Finite strain

The total, integrated deformation recorded by a rock — the difference between its original and final shape, and what you actually measure in the field. Because it is the end product of what may be a long history, finite strain can hide the path taken to get there; that is why we also want incremental strain.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Slip Distribution by Finite Faulting · 2.1 Structural Geology — The Workflow of Tectonics and Structural Geology · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Progressive Deformation

Flexural slip and flexural flow

Two buckling mechanisms that keep layer thickness constant, with zero strain at the hinge increasing down the limbs. Flexural slip is discrete slip on bedding planes, exactly like bending a pile of paper, and leaves slickenlines on bedding surfaces; flexural flow is the same kinematics with the slip smeared into distributed ductile shear within weak layers.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Active Folding or Buckling · 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Processes

Flinn diagram

A plot that classifies every possible strain ellipsoid shape on one graph. Above the diagonal are cigars (constriction, L-tectonites); below it are pancakes (flattening, S-tectonites); the diagonal itself is plane strain. Distance from the origin measures how much strain, the slope measures what kind.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Flinn Diagram

Focal mechanism (beachball)

A representation of how the ground first moved in an earthquake, obtained by dividing the focal sphere into four quadrants with two orthogonal planes and shading the compressional quadrants dark. It shows the fault orientation and slip sense immediately, with the unavoidable ambiguity between the true fault plane and the auxiliary plane, and its P and T axes approximate σ1\sigma_1σ1​ and σ3\sigma_3σ3​.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Stress Vs. Focal Mechanism · 3.1 Stress — Stress Estimates from Focal Mechanism

Fold, accordion

A fold with straight limbs and sharp, angular hinges of near-constant interlimb angle, like the bellows of an accordion. Essentially a synonym for chevron folding where the geometry repeats regularly.

Fold, amplitude

Half the height of a fold measured from the median surface to the hinge — the measure of how far the folding has grown, as distinct from the wavelength, which measures how widely spaced the folds are.

Slides: 5.1 Fold Geometry — Fold styles

Fold, asymmetric

A fold whose two limbs are of unequal length and whose axial surface is inclined, so the fold leans. The direction it leans is its vergence, which is one of the most useful things you can measure in a fold train.

Fold, axial plane

The planar approximation to the axial surface — the surface containing the hinge lines of the folded layers. See axial surface for the more general case.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Bedding / Cleavage relationships

Fold, axial trace

See axial trace: the line of intersection of the axial surface with the ground surface or with any section through the fold.

Fold, axial trend

The compass bearing of the horizontal projection of the fold axis. Together with the plunge it gives the orientation of the axis, and it is what defines the regional structural grain on a map.

Fold, axis

The line that, moved parallel to itself, generates the folded surface — for a cylindrical fold, the direction along which the fold shape does not change. It is a direction rather than a fixed line in space, which is why a stereonet plots it as a single point.

Fold, box

A fold with a broad flat crest and two steep limbs, giving a rectangular profile with two hinges rather than one. Box folds form where shortening cannot be accommodated by a single hinge, often above a rising block or a confined detachment.

Fold, chevron

A fold with straight limbs meeting at a sharp, angular hinge, typically developed in regularly layered rocks with strong competence contrast and high shortening. Chevron folds keep layer thickness constant, so they are accommodated by flexural slip on the bedding planes.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Kinking and Chevron Folding

Fold, close

A fold with an interlimb angle between 30° and 70°. It sits between “open” and “tight” on the standard tightness scale.

Fold, conical

A non-cylindrical fold generated by a line that sweeps out a cone rather than a cylinder, so its profile changes systematically along the hinge and its limbs converge towards an apex. On a stereonet its poles fall on a small circle rather than a great circle.

Fold, conjugate

Paired folds with axial surfaces inclined in opposite directions, formed at the same time under the same shortening — the folded equivalent of a conjugate fault set. Kink bands very often occur in conjugate pairs.

Slides: 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Extensional Faults

Fold, cylindrical

See cylindrical fold: a fold whose surface is generated by moving a straight line parallel to itself, so every profile section is identical.

Fold domain

Part of a fold delimited by inflexion lines. Domains with negative curvature (closing upwards) are antiforms; domains with positive curvature (closing downwards) are synforms.

Fold, en echelon

A set of folds whose individual axes are offset in a stepped, overlapping arrangement, oblique to the trend of the zone as a whole. En echelon folds are characteristic of transpressive shear zones, where the obliquity records the sense of shear.

Fold, eyed

A fold whose hinge zone closes so tightly, and whose limbs are so attenuated, that in section it appears as a closed eye-shaped loop. Eyed folds are a high-strain feature, closely related to sheath folds.

Fold, gentle

A fold with an interlimb angle between 120° and 180° — the most open category, a barely perceptible flexure.

Fold, inclined

A fold whose axial surface dips at an intermediate angle, between upright and recumbent. Fold attitude is classified by the dip of the axial surface and the plunge of the hinge line together.

Fold interference pattern

The geometry produced when a later folding event refolds an earlier one. The four classic patterns — Type 0, Type 1 dome-and-basin, Type 2 mushroom and Type 3 hook — depend on the angles between the two sets of axes and axial planes, and reading them lets you reconstruct the deformation sequence.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Superposed folds - fold interference patterns

Fold, interlimb angle

See interlimb angle: the angle between the two limbs, which measures how tight the fold is.

Fold, isoclinal

A fold with an interlimb angle less than 10°, so the two limbs are effectively parallel. Isoclinal folds indicate very high shortening and are common in high-grade metamorphic terranes.

Fold, kink

A small, sharply hinged fold confined to a narrow band (a kink band) cutting a strongly anisotropic rock such as slate or schist. Kinks form by slip on the pre-existing foliation and often occur in conjugate sets.

Fold, limb

The relatively planar flank of a fold, between one hinge zone and the next. Limb length relative to its neighbour is what defines fold asymmetry.

Fold, neutral

A fold that closes sideways rather than up or down, so it is neither antiformal nor synformal. Neutral folds arise where an earlier fold has been refolded into a steeply plunging orientation.

Fold, non-cylindrical

A fold whose shape changes along its hinge, so it cannot be generated by a single straight line swept parallel to itself. Domes, basins and sheath folds are all non-cylindrical, and on a stereonet their poles scatter off a single great circle.

Fold, open

A fold with an interlimb angle between 70° and 120° — the middle of the tightness scale, indicating modest shortening.

Fold, parasitic

A small, second-order fold developed on the limb or hinge of a larger fold, with a characteristic asymmetry (S, M or Z) that depends on where it sits. Parasitic folds are the practical expression of Pumpelly’s rule: read the small folds and you know the shape of the big one.

Fold, polyclinal

A fold set in which axial surfaces take several different orientations within the same area, rather than the single consistent orientation of a simple fold train. Polyclinal geometry usually means more than one folding event.

Fold, polyharmonic

Folding in which several wavelengths are superimposed on one another, because layers of different thickness in the same sequence each buckle at their own dominant wavelength. It is the natural outcome of Biot’s wavelength selection acting on a multilayer.

Fold, tight

A fold with an interlimb angle between 10° and 30° — nearly closed, just short of isoclinal.

Fold, upright

A fold whose axial surface is vertical or nearly so, giving limbs that dip in opposite directions at similar angles. Upright folds indicate near-horizontal shortening with no strong asymmetry.

Fold, vertical

A fold whose hinge line plunges vertically or nearly so. Vertical fold axes normally result from refolding, since folds rarely nucleate with vertical hinges.

Fold, wavelength

The distance between adjacent hinges of the same sense — the repeat distance of a fold train. In buckling it is set by the layer thickness and the viscosity contrast with the matrix, which is why it is such a useful diagnostic.

Slides: 5.1 Fold Geometry — Fold styles

Fold, zigzag

A fold train of straight limbs and sharp hinges in which successive folds alternate in a regular zigzag, essentially a chevron train of near-symmetric folds.

Foliation

Any penetrative planar or curviplanar fabric in a rock, defined by flattened grains, aligned platy minerals, compositional domains or microfolds. Contrast lineation, which is long in one direction rather than flat in one direction; most deformed metamorphic rocks have both, and the foliation usually approximates the XY plane of the finite strain ellipsoid.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Gneissic Foliation · 5.5 Shear Zones — Foliation in simple shear zones · 3.3 Rheology — Effects of Foliation and Crystal Fabric

Footwall

The block beneath a dipping fault surface. Normal faults drop the hanging wall relative to the footwall; reverse and thrust faults push it up. Neither term means anything on a vertical fault.

Foreland

The low-lying outer margin of an orogen, on the far side from the hinterland. Foreland deformation is thin-skinned and strongly localised — nappes and imbricate thrusts detach above a décollement while the local basement stays untouched — and it receives the sediment eroded from the hinterland.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust Faults and Tectonic Units

Foreshock, mainshock, aftershock

The mainshock is the largest event of a sequence, foreshocks precede it and aftershocks follow — labels that can only be assigned with hindsight. Aftershocks cluster on the mainshock rupture area, decay in rate following Omori’s law, and are typically 1–1.5 magnitude units smaller than the mainshock at the top end.

Fracture

A discontinuity across which cohesion has been lost or reduced — a broken surface, though really a very thin zone rather than a mathematical plane. Fractures are finite: they have a trace where they meet the outcrop, a front separating broken from unbroken rock, and a tip where they die out. The difference from a fault is displacement.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Fracture Vs. Fault · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — New Crack or Existing Fracture Slide? · 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Transform Boundary: Fracture Zone Formation

Fracture zone

The inactive scar continuing beyond the ends of an oceanic transform fault, where both sides now belong to the same plate so there is no relative motion. There is still an age contrast across it, and therefore a bathymetric step, which is how you spot fracture zones on a seafloor map.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Fracture Vs. Fault · 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Transform Boundary: Fracture Zone Formation · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — New Crack or Existing Fracture Slide?

Free surface

A boundary not confined by any external stress, and therefore unable to carry shear stress — the Earth’s surface, to a good approximation. This apparently trivial condition is powerful: it forces one principal stress to be vertical near the surface, which is what makes Anderson’s three-way classification of tectonic regimes possible.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Stress Effects from Free Surface · 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — Principal Stresses & Tectonics

Frictional sliding

Movement on a pre-existing surface once the shear stress along it exceeds the frictional resistance. Friction exists because no surface is smooth — contact is only at asperities — and it depends on normal stress rather than on apparent contact area or rock type. A favourably oriented old fracture usually slips before intact rock breaks, so reactivation is cheaper than making a new fault.

Slides: 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Frictional Sliding · 5.5 Shear Zones — Brittle shear zones

Fry method

A strain-analysis technique exploiting the fact that grain centres in an undeformed rock are statistically isotropically spaced. Plot the positions of neighbouring grains around many centres and the resulting central vacancy is a circle if there is no strain, an ellipse if there is.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — The Fry Method for Strain Analysis

Geometric (descriptive) analysis

The accurate description of the present-day shapes, orientations and positions of rock bodies — the map, cross-section and stereonet work everything else is built on. Kinematics and dynamics are attempts to explain what geometric analysis records.

Slides: 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Geometric lineations · 5.1 Fold Geometry — Geometric Aspects of folds

Graben

A down-dropped block bounded on both sides by inward-dipping normal faults, forming the topographic and sedimentary low of a rift. In the idealised graben-and-horst system the opposing faults grow at equal rates, so the blocks neither rotate nor deform internally.

Gravitational collapse

The spreading and extension of an over-thickened orogen once its topographic load exceeds its internal strength — the mountain belt flows outward under its own weight. It can happen while convergence continues, or afterwards, when post-convergent extension commonly reactivates old thrusts as extensional detachments.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Contractional Structures · 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Summary

Griffith crack

The population of tiny pre-existing flaws — microcracks, pores, grain boundaries — present in every real rock, idealised as narrow ellipses. Griffith’s insight was that failure is not the breaking of a perfect lattice but the growth and linkage of the largest, best-oriented flaws, which is also why large rock samples are weaker than small ones.

Slides: 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Griffith’s Failing Criteria

Griffith failure criterion

A failure criterion derived from crack-tip energetics, plotting as a sideways parabola on a Mohr diagram. It predicts tensile cracking well and is a fair approximation at low confining pressure, but it rises too steeply at high confining pressure, where the Coulomb criterion takes over.

Slides: 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Griffith’s Failing Criteria

Gutenberg–Richter law

The frequency–magnitude relation log⁡N=a−bM\log N = a - bMlogN=a−bM, with bbb usually near 1, meaning roughly ten times as many magnitude 4s as magnitude 5s. Practically it says most earthquakes are small and go undetected, so lowering the detection threshold reveals enormous numbers of hidden events.

Half-graben

An asymmetric extensional basin controlled by a single major normal fault on one side, with the hanging wall tilting towards it rather than dropping symmetrically. Most real rift basins are half-grabens, and adjacent ones dipping in opposite directions are commonly linked by transfer faults.

Hanging wall

The block sitting above a dipping fault surface — from the miners’ practice of hanging a lamp on it while standing on the footwall. Every fault name (normal, reverse, thrust) is a statement about which way the hanging wall moved relative to the footwall.

Heave

The horizontal component of dip separation seen on a cross-section. Like throw, it is an apparent quantity read from a two-dimensional view and depends on the section orientation, so do not mistake it for net slip.

Hinge line

The line joining the points of maximum curvature on a folded surface — the crest of the fold in the geometric sense. Its counterpart is the inflexion line, joining the points of zero curvature.

Hinge surface

The surface containing the hinge lines of successive folded layers. In practice it is used interchangeably with the axial surface.

Hinge zone

The region of a fold around the hinge where curvature is high, as opposed to the relatively planar limbs. In chevron folds the hinge zone is very narrow; in rounded folds it is broad.

Slides: 5.1 Fold Geometry — Basic Elements of Fold Morphology

Hinterland

The internal, mountainous core of an orogen, where the wedge is thickest. Hinterland deformation is typically thick-skinned and penetrative, involving basement as well as cover, and it is the erosional source for sediment shed onto the foreland.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust Faults and Tectonic Units

Homogeneous strain

Strain that is the same everywhere in the body: straight lines stay straight, parallel lines stay parallel, and circles become ellipses. It is the assumption behind essentially all strain analysis, and it is scale-dependent — a deformation that looks messy at outcrop scale may be nicely homogeneous averaged over a map.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Homogeneous & Heterogeneous Strain

Hooke’s law

The linear elastic relation, stress proportional to strain — a spring, in one dimension. For an isotropic material the full rank-4 elastic tensor collapses to just two independent constants.

Horse

The smallest individual fault-bounded unit in a thrust pile — a lens of rock cut from the footwall and bounded above by a roof thrust and below by a floor thrust. Stack a series of horses and you have a duplex; splay them upwards from a common décollement and you have an imbricate fan.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust Faults and Tectonic Units

Horst

The block left standing high between two grabens, bounded by outward-dipping normal faults. Horsts and grabens are the surface expression of extension taken up by conjugate normal fault arrays rather than by block rotation.

Hydraulic fracturing

Driving pore fluid pressure up until the rock cracks in tension, either naturally or deliberately to increase permeability near a well. Used as a stress measurement, the pressure that just holds the fracture open equals the minimum horizontal stress.

Slides: 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Hydraulic Fracturing · 3.1 Stress — How We Measure Stress: Hydraulic Fracturing

Hydrostatic stress

Strictly, the pressure at the base of a water column; more loosely, any purely isotropic stress state with no shear components at all. It is used as a reference state alongside lithostatic pressure, and it matters because pore water is held at something close to or above hydrostatic pressure.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Lithostatic and Hydrostatic Stress

Imbricate fan (imbrication zone)

A series of closely spaced thrusts sharing the same dip direction, each carrying a horse up a ramp, all soling into a common basal décollement. They form preferentially in the foreland, and how steep they look at the surface depends on how deeply the structure has been eroded.

Incremental strain

A small individual increment of deformation, which added together over time produce the finite strain. Teasing out increments — from overprinting fabrics, cross-cutting relationships, syn-kinematic minerals — is how you unravel the sequence of events rather than just its sum.

Slides: 2.1 Structural Geology — The Workflow of Tectonics and Structural Geology · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Progressive Deformation

Indentor

A rigid or strong block driven into a weaker deforming region, as India is into Asia. The resulting pattern depends on what confines the soft material, and a strong block on one side with an open boundary on the other produces asymmetric lateral escape.

Indo-Australian plate

The composite plate carrying Australia, formed in the break-up of Gondwana and strictly made up of the Australian, Indian and Capricorn plates separated by zones of diffuse deformation. Its edges include every type of plate boundary, and forces from all of them are transmitted inwards, loading the Australian stress field and causing earthquakes in a supposedly stable continent.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — What you will learn in this module

Inflexion line

The line joining the points of zero curvature on a folded surface — where the curvature changes sign, marking the boundary between one fold domain and the next.

Inflexion surface

The surface containing the inflexion lines of successive folded layers, separating antiformal from synformal domains.

Instantaneous stretching axes (ISA)

The directions of maximum, intermediate and minimum instantaneous stretching rate at a point — what the flow is doing right now, as opposed to the finite strain ellipsoid, which integrates the whole history. In steady simple shear the ISA sit permanently at 45° to the walls while the strain ellipse rotates towards wall-parallelism.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Instantaneous Stretching Axes in Simple Shear zones

Interlimb angle

The angle between the two limbs of a fold, and the standard measure of how tight it is: gentle (120–180°), open (70–120°), close (30–70°), tight (10–30°), isoclinal (under 10°).

Intersection lineation

The line where two planar structures meet, most often bedding and the first cleavage. Because the intersection of bedding with axial planar cleavage parallels the fold axis, it is a cheap way to get the fold plunge at an outcrop with no visible hinge.

Slides: 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Surface lineations

Intraplate deformation (neotectonics)

Active faulting and earthquakes occurring well inside a plate rather than at its boundary — in Australia concentrated in the Flinders Ranges, the southwest seismic zone and the southeast highlands. The rule of thumb is that intraplate seismicity happens where the modern stress field meets inherited structure, so old sutures and orogens get reactivated.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — Where the earthquakes are

Invariants (of a tensor)

Combinations of tensor components that keep the same value however you rotate your coordinate axes, so they describe the physics rather than your bookkeeping. The first invariant is the trace (three times the mean stress); the second measures the magnitude of the deviatoric part and is what global strain-rate maps plot.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Invariants, Measured

Inverted saddle

A saddle-shaped culmination on a folded surface in which the sense of curvature is reversed relative to the normal saddle — the transition point between adjacent domes or basins along a fold train.

Joint

A Mode I opening fracture with no measurable shear offset and no mineral fill, and the commonest brittle structure in near-surface rocks. Contrast a vein, which is the same kind of opening fracture but mineral-filled, and a fault, which by definition has shear displacement.

Slides: 4.2 Joints and Faults — Joint Arrays

Joint set, system and array

A joint set is a group of joints of similar orientation and morphology; two or more sets make a joint system; an array is the whole population in an outcrop. Sets are described as orthogonal (about 90° apart) or conjugate (30–60°) — but that is pure geometry, and non-parallel sets usually formed at different times under different stress states.

Joint spacing

The average perpendicular distance between adjacent joints of a set, strikingly regular in layered rocks though there is still no agreed mechanism for why. Spacing scales with bed thickness — thin beds are more closely jointed — which is why a layered sequence develops a mechanical stratigraphy.

Slides: 4.2 Joints and Faults — Joint Spacing and Lithology

Kinematic indicator

Any asymmetric structure telling you which wall of a shear zone moved which way: S–C fabrics, mica fish, sigma- and delta-type porphyroclast tails, rotated boudins, deflected markers, asymmetric folds, quartz c-axis fabrics. The working rule is never to trust one alone — look for several, in a section cut parallel to the lineation and perpendicular to the foliation.

Kinematic vorticity number (WkW_kWk​)

A single number measuring how much internal rotation there is relative to how fast strain accumulates, placing a deformation on the spectrum from coaxial to non-coaxial. Wk=0W_k = 0Wk​=0 is pure shear and Wk=1W_k = 1Wk​=1 is simple shear; the paddle-wheel picture helps, with the wheel spinning faster as WkW_kWk​ increases.

Kinematics

The description of how rocks moved and distorted — velocities, displacements, fault slip, rotations and strain histories — without asking what forces caused it. It is the most tractable of the three analyses because you can measure it directly from geometry, GPS and offset markers.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Transform Boundary: Kinematics · 2.1 Structural Geology — The Workflow of Tectonics and Structural Geology

Lineation

Any fabric element much longer in one direction than in the other two. Three families are worth keeping straight: penetrative lineations that pervade the rock volume (stretching, mineral), surface lineations confined to a surface (slickenlines, mullions), and geometric lineations that are constructed lines rather than objects (fold axes, intersection lineations).

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Tangent-lineation Diagrams · 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Tectonic lineations · 5.5 Shear Zones — The ideal plastic shear zone

Lithosphere

A rheological domain describing the upper layer of the Earth with the most plate-like characteristics. Oceanic lithosphere reaches at most about 100–150 km thick, whereas cratonic continental lithosphere can exceed 200 km — a thick/thin contrast that controls where heat, magmatism and deformation localise.

Lithosphere–asthenosphere boundary (LAB)

The base of the mechanically strong plate, mapped with seismology and, in Australia, with AusLAMP magnetotellurics. It is a strength boundary rather than a compositional one, which is why different techniques can disagree about exactly where it lies.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — Datasets: lithospheric thickness (LAB)

Lithostatic stress

The isotropic reference stress at depth from the weight of the overlying rock column, σv=ρgz\sigma_v = \rho g zσv​=ρgz. For continental crust this gives roughly 26 MPa per kilometre of depth — about 130 MPa at 5 km and over 1 GPa at the base of the crust. Tectonic stresses are comparatively small differences added on top of this large background, and it is the differences that break rocks.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Lithostatic and Hydrostatic Stress

Magnetic anomaly stripes

The symmetric pattern of alternating magnetic polarity bands either side of a ridge axis, produced when steadily spreading new crust records the reversals of the Earth’s magnetic field. Read outwards, the stripes are a tape recording of the age of the seafloor and hence of the spreading rate — the observation that turned continental drift into plate tectonics.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — …is not a line

Maxwell time (tM=η/Et_M = \eta/EtM​=η/E)

The timescale deciding whether a material behaves elastically or viscously: deform it faster than tMt_MtM​ and it acts like a solid, slower and it flows. For mantle values it is around a thousand years, which is why the mantle transmits seismic waves elastically yet convects like a fluid over geological time.

Mean stress (σm\sigma_mσm​)

The average of the three normal stresses — the isotropic pressure part of the stress tensor, and its first invariant. It changes a body’s volume but not its shape, and it is the same whatever coordinate system you choose.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Deviatoric Stress and Mean Stress

Median surface

The surface passing through the inflexion lines of a folded layer, midway between the antiformal and synformal hinges. Fold amplitude is measured from it.

Megathrust

The huge, gently dipping thrust forming the interface between the downgoing and overriding plates at a subduction zone — the largest fault surface on Earth and the source of every magnitude 9 earthquake. A wide, strongly coupled, long locked segment that ruptures for hundreds of kilometres with tens of metres of slip is what makes an event like Tohoku possible.

Metamorphic core complex

A dome of deep, high-grade metamorphic rock brought to the surface beneath a low-angle extensional detachment, with much cooler, unmetamorphosed upper-crustal rocks lying directly on top. It is the extreme simple-shear case of extension: the upper plate is stripped off and thinned while the Moho is pulled up beneath.

Slides: 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Summary

Mineral lineation

A linear alignment of minerals or mineral aggregates produced by rigid rotation of elongate grains, by growth of new elongate minerals in a preferred direction, or by dissolution and reprecipitation. Rotation usually deforms the grains and rarely achieves perfect parallelism, whereas syntectonic growth can — that is how you tell the two apart.

Mode I, II and III fractures

The three crack-tip displacement modes: Mode I opens (walls move apart perpendicular to the surface), Mode II slides in-plane, Mode III tears out of plane. They are defined by infinitesimal displacements right at the crack tip, not by the metres of offset you can measure on a fault. Because Mode I cracks open normal to σ3\sigma_3σ3​, joints and veins are good palaeostress indicators.

Moho

The seismically sharp boundary between crust and mantle — the base of the crust, not the base of the plate. Oceanic crust is almost always 6–7 km thick; in Australia the AusMoho model shows 25 km under thinned margins to over 50 km under the cratons, with sharp steps often marking province sutures.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — Datasets: Moho depth

Mohr circle

A graphical device plotting normal stress against shear stress for planes of every possible orientation through a point — a circle centred at the mean of σ1\sigma_1σ1​ and σ3\sigma_3σ3​ with radius half their difference. Every point on the circle is one plane; watch the factor of two, since an angle θ\thetaθ in the rock appears as 2θ2\theta2θ on the circle. It turns a tensor problem into something you can read with a ruler.

Moment magnitude (MwM_wMw​)

The magnitude scale computed directly from seismic moment, and the only one that stays meaningful for great earthquakes. It is why Tohoku is quoted as MwM_wMw​ 9.0–9.1 when the band-limited magnitudes available in the first minutes badly underestimated it.

Moment tensor

The tensor quantity a beachball represents, encoding the magnitude and orientation of slip at the source. In this course we assume a double-couple mechanism — slip on a plane with no volume change — which is a simplification of real observed moment tensors.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — What the crust is worth · 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Focal Mechanisms 2

Monocline

A local step-like flexure in otherwise gently dipping or flat strata, with one steep limb between two shallow ones. Monoclines commonly form by bending of cover rocks above a reactivated basement fault.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Bending or forced folding

Mullion

A large, corrugated, columnar structure developed at an interface between rocks of strongly contrasting viscosity — classically quartzite against phyllite — forming a coarse surface lineation parallel to the fold axis. Think of it as the outcrop-scale expression of an interface buckling instability.

Mylonite

A strongly foliated, fine-grained rock formed by intense plastic deformation in the core of a ductile shear zone, in which pre-existing structures are flattened, transposed and hard to identify. The grain-size reduction is by dynamic recrystallisation, not crushing — the key contrast with cataclasite, which is comminuted rather than recrystallised.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Cleavage development

Nappe

A large, far-travelled sheet of rock emplaced along a thrust, typically very thin relative to its lateral extent and wedge-shaped in section. A thrust nappe is made of one or more subordinate thrust sheets sharing a common displacement history; the Alps are the classic nappe pile.

Net slip

The vector joining two originally adjacent points on opposite walls of a fault — the true displacement, resolvable into dip-slip and strike-slip components. It can only be measured where you can find a piercing point, and it is independent of which side of the fault you stand on.

Newtonian (linear) viscosity

The simple case where stress is directly proportional to strain rate, so viscosity is a constant independent of how hard you push. Water and honey are Newtonian; it is the assumption behind simple mantle-flow calculations, but only a first approximation for rock.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Viscous Deformation

Non-coaxial deformation

Deformation in which the principal strain axes rotate relative to the material as strain accumulates, so the orientation of the finite strain ellipsoid depends on how much strain you have. Simple shear is the type example, and it is why shear-zone fabrics rotate progressively towards the shear plane.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Pure Shear and Simple Shear

Normal fault

A dip-slip fault on which the hanging wall moves down relative to the footwall, so the reference surface is lengthened. Normal faults are how the brittle upper 10–15 km of crust accommodates extension; below that, extension is taken up on low-angle ductile shear zones.

Slides: 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Normal Fault systems · 3.1 Stress — Implication to Normal/reverse Faulting · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Implication to Normal/reverse Faulting

Normal stress (σn\sigma_nσn​)

The component of traction acting perpendicular to a plane — the part that squeezes it shut or pulls it open. On a fault it controls the frictional resistance, since friction scales with how hard the two walls are pressed together.

Slides: 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Normal Fault systems · 3.1 Stress — Implication to Normal/reverse Faulting · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Implication to Normal/reverse Faulting

Omori’s law

The empirical rule that aftershock rate decays roughly as one over the time since the mainshock. It explains why hazard is greatest in the hours and days after a big earthquake and falls off quickly thereafter — but never quite to zero.

Orogen

A belt of crust built or reworked by a mountain-building event. In Australia orogens are the mortar joining the cratonic blocks — Capricorn, Albany–Fraser, Musgrave, Arunta — and the continent also has intracontinental orogens (Petermann, Alice Springs) recording mountain building far from any plate boundary.

Orogenic wedge

The wedge-shaped fold-and-thrust belt built during crustal contraction, thickest in the hinterland and tapering towards the foreland, translated forward along a basal detachment. The standard analogy is snow piling up in front of a bulldozer.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Orogenic Wedges

Outer rise

The broad, gentle bulge on the seaward side of a trench, where the incoming plate flexes upward before plunging. The top of the bending plate is in extension, so the outer rise is covered with normal faults and produces normal-faulting earthquakes even though the whole setting is convergent.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Outer Rise Earthquakes

Overcoring

A strain-relaxation method of measuring in-situ stress: instrument a small pilot hole, then drill a larger core around it so the rock cylinder is released from the surrounding stress field and springs back. The recovered strains are converted to stresses using the rock’s elastic moduli — so it measures strain and infers stress.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — How We Measure Stress: Overcoring and Geological Structure

Overriding plate

The upper plate at a subduction zone, dragged down and bent by coupling to the descending slab during the locked phase and rebounding when the megathrust fails. Because this is the part that is above sea level and populated, its flexure controls uplift, subsidence and tsunami hazard.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Stresses / Bending in the Overriding Plate

P, T and N axes

The pressure, tension and null axes plotted on a beachball, taken as proxies for the maximum, minimum and intermediate principal stress directions. Watch the notation trap: the P axis sits in the light region near σ1\sigma_1σ1​ and the T axis in the dark region near σ3\sigma_3σ3​, and they are only approximations because faults do not form at exactly 45° to the principal stresses.

Parasitic fold

See “Fold, parasitic”.

Passive folding

Folding in which the layers play no mechanical role and simply act as passive markers carried by the flow — the card-deck model. Contrast buckling: passive folding needs no competence contrast, dominates at high temperature and in shear zones, and produces similar folds of constant thickness parallel to the axial surface.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Passive Folding · 1.1a The Australian Plate — The Wilson Cycle

Passive margin

A continental margin that is not a plate boundary, formed by rifting and subsequent thermal subsidence, so it accumulates thick sediment wedges but has little seismicity. Australia’s southern and western margins are the type examples.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Passive Folding · 1.1a The Australian Plate — The Wilson Cycle

Pencil cleavage

The intermediate stage in which a weak tectonic cleavage is about as well expressed as the bedding it cuts at a high angle, so the rock breaks along both at once into pencil-shaped splinters. A handy field marker for early, low-strain shortening in a mudrock.

Perfect plasticity

The idealisation in which deformation continues indefinitely at exactly the yield stress, with no further increase or decrease needed — the flat-topped stress–strain curve that strain hardening and softening deviate from.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Linear and Non-linear Elasticity

Plastic deformation

Permanent change of shape without fracture, beginning only once the stress exceeds a yield point and then continuing at roughly constant stress. The mechanical analogue is a block sliding on a frictional surface: nothing happens until the threshold is passed, then it just goes.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Plastic Deformation · 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Lineations related to plastic deformation · 5.5 Shear Zones — Plastic Shear zones

Plate boundary

The rigid plates move relative to one another without deforming, except in the very narrow accommodation zones at their edges known as plate boundaries. There are three main types — convergent, divergent and transform — each with its own kinematic rules and internal structure.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Plate Boundary Map · 1.1a The Australian Plate — A tour of the boundaries

Plate boundary (convergent)

A boundary where two plates move towards each other and material is consumed or thickened — by subduction of oceanic lithosphere at a trench, or by collision where buoyant continental crust refuses to sink and the boundary jams instead.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Plate Boundary Map · 1.1a The Australian Plate — A tour of the boundaries

Plate boundary (divergent)

A boundary where two plates move apart and new lithosphere is created between them, most obviously at mid-ocean ridges. Its continental equivalent is a rift, which may or may not go on to become a spreading centre.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Plate Boundary Map · 1.1a The Australian Plate — A tour of the boundaries

Plate boundary (transform)

A boundary where two plates slide past one another and lithosphere is neither created nor destroyed. Oceanic transforms link offset ridge segments and trace small circles about the Euler pole of the plate pair, which is why they are orthogonal to the ridges they offset.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Plate Boundary Map · 1.1a The Australian Plate — A tour of the boundaries

Plate coupling

The fraction of the long-term plate convergence rate not being taken up by aseismic creep on the plate interface — the fraction stored as elastic strain for future earthquakes. Coupling maps derived from GPS are the main tool for identifying which megathrust segments are locked and loading.

Plate (tectonic plate)

A fragment of the Earth’s lithosphere that is strong and moves like a rigid block on the surface. Implicitly this means the oceanic lithosphere, because continental lithosphere is often much weaker and deformable.

Plumose structure

The faint feathery ornament on a joint face — a plume axis with hackles fanning away from it — recording the propagation direction and pointing back to the nucleation point. Related features are arrest lines, marking pauses in growth, and twist hackles at the fringe where the crack tries to reorient into a rotated σ3\sigma_3σ3​.

Poisson’s ratio (ν\nuν)

The ratio of sideways bulging to axial shortening when you squeeze something — how much a material fattens as it is compressed. For Earth materials it lies between 0 and 0.5, typically 0.15–0.35; a value of 0.5 means perfectly incompressible.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Poisson’s Ratio

Pop-up structure

The block of rock squeezed upward between bounding reverse faults at a restraining bend or contractional stepover. Scale it up in nature, add erosion, and you get a mountain range sitting astride a strike-slip fault.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Fault Bends and Stepovers

Pore fluid pressure (pfp_fpf​)

The pressure of the fluid filling a rock’s pore space, pushing outward against the grains. Because it holds the grain contacts apart, it reduces the stress genuinely carried by the rock framework — which is why raising fluid pressure, naturally or by injection, can trigger faulting.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Presence of Fluids

Power-law creep

The real behaviour of rock at depth, where strain rate goes as stress to a power (typically 3–5 for olivine), so the effective viscosity drops as you push harder. This makes deformation self-focusing: fast-deforming regions weaken and take up even more of the strain, which is exactly why plate boundaries are narrow.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Viscous Deformation

Pressure solution

Dissolution of minerals at grain contacts oriented at a high angle to σ1\sigma_1σ1​, diffusion of the dissolved material along fluid films on grain boundaries, and reprecipitation in low-stress sites such as pressure shadows and veins — or removal from the rock entirely. It is the dominant foliation-forming process at low metamorphic grade, and it can account for very large shortening.

Primary structure

A structure formed at the time the rock itself formed, by sedimentary or magmatic processes — bedding, cross-bedding, flow banding. Structural geology is about the tectonic structures that came later, but you cannot recognise deformation without first knowing what the undeformed rock looks like.

Slides: 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Primary or non-tectonic lineations · 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Primary foliation vs Secondary foliation

Principal strains

The three mutually perpendicular directions in which lines are only stretched or shortened but not sheared — the axes of the strain ellipsoid. Careful: the principal strain axes need not coincide with the principal stress axes unless the deformation is coaxial and the material isotropic.

Slides: 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — Principal Stresses · 3.1 Stress — Principal Stresses · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Principal Strain: Strain Ellipsoid

Principal stresses (σ1≥σ2≥σ3\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3σ1​≥σ2​≥σ3​)

The three special, mutually perpendicular directions in which the shear stresses vanish and only pushes or pulls remain — the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the stress tensor, ordered from most to least compressive. Their orientation and relative size is the single most useful description of a tectonic stress state, and which faults form and which slip follows from it.

Slides: 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — Principal Stresses · 3.1 Stress — Principal Stresses · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Principal Strain: Strain Ellipsoid

Progressive deformation

The accumulation of strain through a sequence of increments, in which the finite strain at any moment is the integrated result. A given finite strain can be reached in an infinite number of ways, so the rock has effectively forgotten its path — which is why strain alone rarely tells you the stress history.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Progressive Deformation · 2.1 Structural Geology — Range of methods to approach structures

Pseudotachylyte

A dark, glassy fault rock formed by frictional melting during a single seismic slip event — “pseudo” plus tachylyte, a basaltic glass, and nothing to do with volcanism. It requires very fast, high-stress slip, and so is treasured as a fossil record of an ancient earthquake.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Pseudotachylyte

Pull-apart basin

The rhomb-shaped structural low that opens at a releasing bend or stepover as two overlapping strike-slip segments pull the intervening block apart. They subside fast and can get very deep for their size — the Dead Sea and Death Valley are the standard examples.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Releasing Bends

Pumpelly’s rule

The observation that the geometry and style of small-scale structures generally mirror those of the larger structures containing them. It is what licenses you to measure a few minor folds at an outcrop and infer the regional fold geometry — a rule of thumb, not a law.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Pumpelly’s Rule · 2.1 Structural Geology — Structural Geology and Crustal deformation

Pure shear

A coaxial deformation in which material shortens in one direction and stretches perpendicular to it without bulk rotation. Squash a block vertically and it thickens horizontally, but the principal axes stay put. In rifting, pure shear means symmetrical, roughly vertical thinning of the lithosphere.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Pure Shear · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Pure Shear and Simple Shear · 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Orogenic Wedges

Ramp-flat geometry

The staircase trajectory a thrust follows as it propagates, running along bedding through weak layers (flats) and cutting up-section through stiffer layers (ramps). Because the hanging wall has to bend as it rides over each ramp, ramp-flat geometry is also the origin of much of the folding seen in thrust belts.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust systems geometries

Ramps: frontal, lateral and oblique

Ramps classified by orientation relative to the transport direction — frontal ramps are perpendicular to transport and take reverse dip-slip, lateral ramps are parallel to transport and behave as near-vertical transfer faults, and oblique ramps take oblique slip. Recognising which you are looking at immediately gives the transport direction.

Releasing bend

A bend or stepover along a strike-slip fault where the geometry pulls the two sides apart, creating local extension, normal faulting, subsidence and a negative flower structure. Whether a bend restrains or releases depends jointly on its geometry and the sense of slip, so you must know both.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Releasing Bends

Restraining bend

A bend or stepover along a strike-slip fault where the geometry forces the two sides together, creating local contraction — folds, reverse faults, positive flower structures, uplifted pop-up blocks, and if the bend is big enough, a mountain range.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Fault Bends and Stepovers · 3.1 Stress — Now run it backwards

Reverse fault

A dip-slip fault steeper than about 30° on which the hanging wall moves up relative to the footwall, accommodating horizontal shortening. Think of it as the steep sibling of a thrust: the 30° cut-off is a convention, not a change of mechanism.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrusts Faults and reverse Faults · 3.1 Stress — Implication to Normal/reverse Faulting · 4.1 Brittle Deformation — Implication to Normal/reverse Faulting

Rf/ϕR_f/\phiRf​/ϕ method

A strain-analysis method for populations of sub-spherical objects that already had a spread of initial shapes. You measure each object’s final axial ratio against its long-axis orientation, and the shape of the resulting cloud lets you extract the true tectonic strain ratio from the initial scatter.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — The Fry Method for Strain Analysis

Rheological stratification

The layering of the lithosphere into alternating strong and weak levels, because different minerals control the strength at different depths — quartz in the upper crust, feldspar deeper, olivine in the mantle. Each has its own brittle–plastic crossover, giving the familiar “jelly sandwich” strength profile.

Rheology

The study of how a material deforms or flows in response to stress — the constitutive law linking stress to strain (elastic), to strain rate (viscous), or to a strength limit (plastic). The same rock can do all three; which one wins depends on temperature, pressure, fluids and how fast you push it.

Slides: 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — Rheology · 3.3 Rheology — Rheology

Ridge push

The gravitational body force arising because young lithosphere at a ridge sits high and old lithosphere sits deep, so the whole cooling plate slides gently away from the elevated axis. It is a distributed consequence of the age–depth relationship, not a shove applied at the ridge axis.

Riedel (R) and P shears

The small en echelon shear fractures appearing early in a developing strike-slip zone, oriented at characteristic angles to the eventual through-going fault, whose linkage produces the master fault. They show that a strike-slip fault begins as an array of small structures rather than a single clean break.

Rift basin

An elongate extensional basin formed where continental lithosphere is being stretched and thinned, typically evolving through early fracturing, a main stretching phase with well-developed normal faults, and a post-rift phase of thermal subsidence. In active rifting the driver is a rising hot mantle upwelling; in passive rifting it is far-field plate stress localising on pre-existing weaknesses.

Rollover fold

The gentle anticlinal flexure formed in the hanging wall of a listric normal fault, as the hanging wall bends to stay in contact with the curved fault surface. It is one of the ways nature avoids opening a gap in extension — the alternatives being antithetic or synthetic faulting within the hanging wall.

Roof thrust and floor thrust

The bounding faults of a duplex or nappe — the floor (or sole) thrust at the base and the roof thrust at the top. Slip arriving on the floor thrust has to go somewhere, and transferring it to the roof thrust is what allows a stack of horses to be built between them.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust Faults and Tectonic Units

Saddle

The col-shaped low point on a folded surface between two culminations, where the surface curves upward in one direction and downward in the other.

S–C fabric

The pair of intersecting foliations in a mylonite: S surfaces track the flattening plane and curve in and out of C surfaces lying parallel to the shear-zone boundary. The sense in which S is deflected into C gives the shear sense directly — but C surfaces form relatively late, so they may record only the last stage of deformation.

Scalar, vector, tensor

A scalar has magnitude only (temperature, mass); a vector has magnitude and one direction (force, velocity); a second-order tensor has magnitude and two directions — one for the plane it acts on and one for the direction of action (stress, strain, permeability). Remember that a tensor is a machine that eats a plane orientation and returns a vector, and the algebra stops being mysterious.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Scalar, Vector & Tensor

Schistosity

The coarser, visibly crystalline foliation of a schist, developed at greenschist facies and above when new micas grow large enough to see in hand specimen. Contrast cleavage: cleavage forms mainly by pressure solution and rotation at low grade and defines a splitting surface, whereas schistosity is dominated by recrystallisation and is defined by aligned micas and elongate quartz.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Schistosity

Seafloor spreading

The creation of new oceanic lithosphere at a ridge axis and its symmetric transport away on both sides, so the ocean floor is a conveyor belt with the youngest rock at the ridge. Confirming it in the late 1950s and early 1960s is what turned continental drift into plate tectonics.

Seismic moment

The physical measure of earthquake size — rigidity times rupture area times average slip, or how much rock slipped how far. It has units of energy and, unlike the older amplitude-based magnitudes, it does not saturate for the largest events.

Seismic vs aseismic slip

Seismic slip is sudden and radiates elastic waves — an earthquake; aseismic slip, or fault creep, is slow continuous movement that releases strain without radiating waves. The same fault can do both in different places or at different times, which is why geodesy as well as seismology is needed to balance a fault’s slip budget.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Seismic Derivation · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Example: Strain from a Seismic Wave · 1.1a The Australian Plate — …is not a line

Sense of shear

Which wall of a shear zone moved which way, read from kinematic indicators. Determining it is the whole point of shear-zone microstructural analysis, and it must be done on a section cut parallel to the lineation and perpendicular to the foliation.

Shear fracture

A fracture with a small (millimetre to decimetre) shear offset, typically forming at 20–30° to σ1\sigma_1σ1​ and often in conjugate pairs. Triaxial experiments show it develops through closure of existing cracks, elastic response, new microcrack growth, then coalescence of those microcracks into a through-going surface.

Shear modulus (μ\muμ, GGG)

The elastic stiffness in shear — resistance to change of shape at constant volume. It controls S-wave speed and earthquake moment. Note the notation clash: μ\muμ is also used for friction and sometimes for viscosity.

Shear strain (γ\gammaγ)

A measure of how much angles have changed. In a shear zone it is simply offset divided by zone width, so 5 cm of offset across a 0.5 cm zone gives γ=10\gamma = 10γ=10.

Shear stress (τ\tauτ, σs\sigma_sσs​)

The component of traction acting along a plane — the part trying to slide one side past the other. For a uniaxial load, shear stress is greatest on planes at 45° to the load, which is why things tend to break obliquely rather than straight across.

Slides: 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — Watching stress choose the faults

Shear zone

A tabular zone in which strain is markedly higher than in the surrounding rock — the classic expression of strain localisation, and the deeper-crustal relative of a fault. Crucially a shear zone has a thickness significant relative to its displacement, whereas an idealised fault surface has none.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Shear zone: Definition

Sheath fold

An extremely non-cylindrical fold whose hinge has been rotated towards the transport direction until it closes like the finger of a glove, producing eye-shaped patterns in cross-section. Sheath folds are a high-strain, strongly non-coaxial signature: they tell you that you are in a shear zone.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Sheath folds

Simple shear

A non-coaxial deformation like shearing a deck of cards, in which parallel planes stay parallel and a constant distance apart while sliding past one another, and material lines rotate progressively. It is the idealisation for shear zones and strike-slip deformation.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Sub-Simple Shear · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Pure Shear and Simple Shear · 2.1 Structural Geology — The Workflow of Tectonics and Structural Geology

Slab

The tongue of subducted oceanic lithosphere descending into the mantle, imaged by the depth distribution of earthquakes and by tomography. It is a sheet rather than a cylinder, which is why slabs drive much larger mantle flow than plumes do.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Slab Rollback

Slab pull

The gravitational force exerted by cold, dense subducted lithosphere sinking into the mantle and dragging the attached surface plate behind it. It is generally taken to be the dominant plate driving force, which is why the fastest-moving plates are those with the longest subducting margins.

Slab rollback

The seaward migration of the trench itself as the slab sinks and hinges backward, so the trench moves relative to both plates. Two subduction zones with identical convergence rates can put very different amounts of material into the mantle depending on rollback.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Slab Rollback

Slaty cleavage

The strong, penetrative, closely spaced cleavage of a slate, formed once continued shortening makes the tectonic fabric dominant. It develops by grain rotation, oriented mineral growth and above all pressure solution, which segregates the rock into quartz-feldspar and mica domains.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Development of Secondary foliations

Slickensides and slip lineations

A slickenside is a fault surface polished by frictional sliding; the grooves, striae and fibrous mineral growths on it are slickenlines recording the direction of the last movement. They give the slip direction reliably but not the sense — for that you need steps or fibre asymmetry — and two sets on one surface mean two slip episodes.

Slides: 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Slickensides and Slip Lineations

Stepover

A place where slip steps sideways from one strike-slip fault segment to an overlapping neighbour rather than continuing on a single trace. Stepovers arise as faults grow by linking segments, and like bends they are either contractional or extensional depending on the step direction relative to the slip sense.

Strain / strain-rate

Strain is the relative change in shape or size of a body — dimensionless, often quoted as a percentage — while strain rate is how fast that strain accumulates, in units of s−1^{-1}−1. Strain tells you what happened in total; strain rate tells you how quickly it is happening now. Plate-boundary zones run at about 10-15 to 10-14 s−1^{-1}−1, plate interiors a thousand times slower.

Strain ellipsoid

The shape an originally spherical marker takes after homogeneous strain, with axes along the principal strain directions. It represents the same state of strain whatever coordinate system you choose, and its shape controls the resulting fabric — pancakes give schistosity, cigars give lineation.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Principal Strain: Strain Ellipsoid

Strain hardening

Behaviour in which rock gets stronger as it deforms, so more stress is needed to keep it going. It is favoured by high confining pressure, which makes fractures harder to open and keeps the rock coherent.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Strain Hardening and Softening

Strain markers

Objects in a rock whose original shape you can reasonably guess, so that their present shape records the strain — deformed pebbles, ooids, mineral grains, pillow lavas, worm burrows, trilobites. Without a marker you can measure geometry but not strain.

Strain partitioning

The tendency for oblique convergence to be split between separate structures rather than every structure doing a bit of everything — typically a strike-slip component localised onto discrete faults and a fault-perpendicular shortening component distributed as folds and thrusts between them. It reflects the general habit of simple shear localising while pure shear stays distributed.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Strain Partitioning

Strain softening

Behaviour in which less stress is needed to continue deformation once it has started, so deformation localises into narrow zones. High temperature, more fluid, lower strain rate and grain-size reduction all promote it — which is why shear zones and faults, once formed, keep being used.

Slides: 3.3 Rheology — Strain Hardening and Softening

Strain tensor

The array built from the symmetric part of the displacement gradients. Diagonal terms are stretches along the axes and off-diagonal terms are shears; like stress it is symmetric and can be diagonalised. The simple form assumes small strain.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Strain Tensor

Stress

Force per unit area acting across a surface inside a material, in pascals. Unlike a force, stress depends on the orientation of the surface you imagine cutting, so a full description at a point needs a tensor rather than a single number or arrow. A useful anchor: the weight of the overburden gives about 26 MPa for every kilometre of depth.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Stress · 1.1 Global Deformation Patterns — Global Stress · 1.3 Stress, Strain and Strength — What is Stress ?

Stress concentration

The amplification of remote stress at the tip of a flaw, which resolves the paradox that rocks fail at around 10 MPa when atomic bond strength predicts thousands. Long, thin, sharp cracks are by far the most effective stress amplifiers.

Stress drop

The shear stress released on a fault by an earthquake, typically only 1–10 MPa — a small fraction of the roughly 80 MPa needed to make the fault slip in the first place. Earthquakes therefore relieve only a little of the accumulated stress, and faults stay close to their failure limit.

Stress ellipsoid

The geometric picture of a stress state: the three principal stresses drawn as mutually perpendicular axes, with the resulting ellipsoid capturing magnitude and orientation together. A sphere means pure pressure; a flattened or elongate ellipsoid means strong deviatoric stress.

Stress tensor

The array σij\sigma_{ij}σij​ storing the complete state of stress at a point — three traction vectors acting on three mutually perpendicular faces of a tiny cube. It is symmetric, because otherwise the cube would spin, so only six of the nine numbers are independent. It is just a machine that takes the orientation of a plane and hands back the force on it.

Stress trajectories

Smooth flow lines interpolated through scattered stress-orientation measurements to show the continuous pattern of the compressional stress direction across a region. Useful where data are sparse, as in Australia, but remember they are an interpolation.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Stress trajectories

Stretch (sss)

The ratio of final to original length, so s=1s = 1s=1 means no deformation. Principal strains are often quoted as stretches, and the quadratic elongation (stretch squared) turns up in older formulations.

Slides: 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Longitudinal Strain

Stretching lineation

A penetrative lineation defined by objects plastically stretched into rods, marking the direction of greatest extension. It is the most useful lineation for regional tectonics because in a thrust belt or shear zone it approximates the transport direction.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Instantaneous Stretching Axes in Simple Shear zones · 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Tectonic lineations

Strike-slip fault

A fault whose displacement is essentially horizontal, so the two blocks slide past one another sideways. They are typically steep, can be hundreds of kilometres long, accumulate large total displacement, and occur in every tectonic regime — which is why they produce some of the world’s most damaging earthquakes.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Strike-slip faults · 1.1a The Australian Plate — The stress field that drives them

Stylolite

A rough, serrated, tooth-like seam produced where rock has dissolved under stress, leaving insoluble residue that picks the surface out as a dark wiggly line. Stylolites are contractional, and their teeth point along σ1\sigma_1σ1​ — so bedding-parallel stylolites record burial loading while oblique ones record tectonic stress.

Slides: 5.3 Structures Associated with Folding (1) — Pressure solution

Subduction zone

A convergent boundary where oceanic lithosphere bends down at a trench and is recycled into the mantle, carrying its crust, sediments and water with it. It is inherently three-dimensional and mechanically dominated by the megathrust contact between the two plates.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Subduction zone stresses · 4.3 Faults and Fault Zones — Earthquakes at Subduction Zones · 1.1a The Australian Plate — The Wilson Cycle

Sub-simple shear

The realistic case in which a shear zone combines simple shear with a pure-shear component, so the kinematic vorticity number lies between 0 and 1. The diagnostic is the instantaneous stretching axes: in sub-simple shear they are oblique to the walls at an angle other than 45° and stay fixed during steady deformation.

Slides: 5.5 Shear Zones — Sub-Simple Shear · 3.2 Strain and Strain Rate — Quantify Strain with Displacement: in Two Dimensions

Supercontinent cycle

The long rhythm underneath the Wilson cycle: most of the continental crust gathers into a single mass and then disperses again, roughly every 500–700 million years. The assemblies usually named are Nuna (also called Columbia, ~1.8 Ga), Rodinia (~1.0 Ga), and Gondwana merging into Pangea (~0.3 Ga). Australia was inside all of them, which is why its assembly history is told against those names.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — Two bursts, not a steady drip

Superposed folding

What you get when a later deformation phase refolds an earlier generation, giving refolded folds and characteristic interference patterns. Reading those patterns lets you reconstruct the deformation sequence.

Slides: 5.2 Folds and Folding Mechanisms — Superposed folds - fold interference patterns

Syncline

A fold that closes downwards and has the youngest rocks in its core — the stratigraphic counterpart of a synform, usable only when you know the way-up of the beds.

Slides: 5.1 Fold Geometry — Syncline

Synclinorium

A large composite syncline: a regional downward-closing structure whose limbs and hinge carry many smaller folds.

Synform

A fold that closes downwards, described purely by geometry. Use it when the way-up of the beds is unknown or when they are overturned.

Syntaxial and antitaxial veins

In a syntaxial vein crystals grow inwards from both walls in continuity with the wall rock, so the youngest material is the median line in the centre; in an antitaxial vein growth is outwards from a central seam, so the centre is oldest. Reading the growth direction lets you unpick the opening history.

Synthetic and antithetic

A structure is synthetic if it shares the prevailing sense of shear or dip of the system, and antithetic if it opposes it. In the hanging wall above a listric normal fault, synthetic minor faults dip the same way as the master fault while antithetic ones dip back against it.

Slides: 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Extensional Faults

Tasman Line

The conventional boundary between the Precambrian cratonic core of Australia and the accreted Phanerozoic Tasmanides to the east. It is useful shorthand and does echo real steps in Moho depth and lithospheric thickness, but its exact position is actively debated and it is certainly not a single line.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — The Tasman Line

Tasmanides

The Phanerozoic accreted eastern third of the Australian continent — the Delamerian, Lachlan, Thomson, Mossman and New England orogens, added successively along the Gondwana margin during the Palaeozoic. Their lithosphere is much thinner than the cratons to the west, which is why they still localise heat, magmatism and deformation.

Slides: 1.1a The Australian Plate — The names of things

Tectonic regime

The classification of a stress state by which principal stress is vertical: normal faulting when σ1\sigma_1σ1​ is vertical, strike-slip when σ2\sigma_2σ2​ is vertical, thrust when σ3\sigma_3σ3​ is vertical. Because the Earth’s surface is a free surface, one principal stress must be near-vertical — which is what makes this three-way classification possible.

Slides: 2.3 Extensional Regimes — Tectonic Extension · 5.4 Structures Associated with Folding (2) — Tectonic lineations · 3.1 Stress — Major Tectonic Forces

Thick-skinned deformation

Deformation involving basement and cover together, typical of an orogenic hinterland. Whether basement is in the section changes the cross-section balance completely, so deciding between thick- and thin-skinned is one of the first interpretive judgements you make.

Thin-skinned deformation

Deformation involving only the sedimentary cover, which slides on a décollement while the basement below stays undeformed — typical of a foreland thrust belt.

Throw

The vertical component of dip separation seen on a cross-section. Like heave it is an apparent quantity depending on the section orientation, so do not mistake it for net slip.

Thrust fault

A low-angle contractional fault, dipping less than about 30°, along which the hanging wall has been carried up and over the footwall, often for tens of kilometres. Because they carry deep rocks over shallow ones, thrusts characteristically place older rocks on younger and higher metamorphic grade on lower — which is how you map them.

Slides: 2.2 Contractional Regimes — Thrust Faults · 1.1a The Australian Plate — The stress field that drives them

Traction

The force per unit area actually acting on one particular plane through a point — a vector, with a size and a direction. You get it from the stress tensor and the plane’s unit normal. Traction is what you feel on a fault; the stress tensor is what generates it for every possible fault orientation.

Slides: 3.1 Stress — Example: Computing Traction Vector

Transcurrent fault

A regional strike-slip fault in continental crust that is not bounded by extensional or contractional structures, so it has free tips and can grow according to the regional stress field. Contrast transfer and transform faults, whose lengths are fixed by the structures at their ends.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Transcurrent faults

Transfer fault

A strike-slip fault that transfers displacement from one structure to another rather than growing freely. In a rift they link and segment half-grabens; in a thrust belt they carry slip between thrust sheets. They terminate against the structures they serve.

Transform fault

A strike-slip plate boundary along which lithosphere is neither created nor destroyed, most commonly linking offset segments of a mid-ocean ridge. Note the counterintuitive kinematics: the slip sense on the fault is opposite to the apparent offset of the ridge, and beyond the ridge tips the structure continues only as a dead fracture zone.

Transpression

Deformation combining strike-slip motion along a zone with shortening perpendicular to it, so the side walls converge as they slide past each other. It is what you get when a shear zone departs from ideal simple shear, and it is common along real plate boundaries.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Transpression and Transtension

Transtension

Strike-slip motion combined with extension perpendicular to the zone, so the walls pull apart as they slide. Transtensional zones produce normal-fault offsets and subsidence — the mirror image of transpression.

Slides: 2.4 Strike-Slip, Transtension & Transpression — Transpression and Transtension

Trench

The deep bathymetric furrow marking where the downgoing plate flexes and begins its descent — the outermost surface expression of a subduction zone. Trenches are generally curved in plan view, so plate motion is oblique to them along most of their length.

Triple junction

A point where three plates, and therefore three plate boundaries, meet. Their behaviour is severely constrained by kinematics: three ridges are always stable, many ridge–trench–transform combinations work, and some, such as three transforms, are impossible.

Slides: 1.2 Plate Boundaries — Triple Junction Stability

Trough line

The line joining the topographically lowest points on a folded surface — the counterpart of the crest line, and coincident with the hinge line only in an upright fold.

Tsunami run-up and inundation

Run-up is the maximum water height above sea level at the landward limit of a tsunami; inundation is how far inland the water reached. Because tsunamis are generated by vertical displacement of the sea floor, it is slip on the shallow part of the megathrust near the trench — not magnitude alone — that controls tsunami size.

Ultramylonite

The most extreme member of the mylonite series, in which almost the entire rock has been reduced to a fine recrystallised matrix and hardly any original grains survive. It marks the highest-strain heart of a shear zone.

Uniaxial strain (compaction)

Shortening along one principal strain axis with no length change along the other two — necessarily a volume change, since nothing compensates sideways. Sediment compaction is the classic case, with porosity falling as pore fluid is expelled with burial.

Vein

A fracture filled with minerals, usually quartz or calcite, precipitated from solution — so veins are direct evidence that fluid moved through a fracture. Vein arrays take characteristic forms: planar, en echelon (shear-related), and stockwork, from shattering by high fluid pressure.

Velocity gradient

The spatial rate of change of velocity — the raw quantity from which both deformation and rotation are extracted. Its symmetric part is the strain-rate tensor (shape change) and its antisymmetric part is the vorticity (spin), which is exactly the distinction between pure and simple shear.

Vergence

The direction in which a structure leans — the asymmetry of a fold, thrust or shear zone that points towards the direction of tectonic transport. Vergence, fold asymmetry, bedding–cleavage relations and facing all flip together across a major hinge, so pinning down any two constrains the others.

Viscosity (η\etaη)

The resistance of a material to flowing, in pascal seconds. Everyday liquids span 0.001 to 100 Pa s; ice is around 1013; the mantle is 1018 to 1024, with 1021 a good round default. Viscosity only ever resists the formation of velocity gradients — it is never a driving force.

Viscous deformation

Irreversible flow in which stress depends on strain rate rather than on strain. Push harder and it flows faster; stop pushing and it simply stops, keeping all the deformation it has accumulated. This is how rocks behave over geological time at depth, flowing in the solid state by the migration of crystal defects.

Volcanic arc

The curved chain of volcanoes built on the overriding plate, roughly parallel to and about 100 km above the slab, fed by melting driven by water released from the descending plate. The curvature of arcs and trenches is a signature that subduction zones are not simple two-dimensional structures.

Vorticity

The rotational part of the velocity gradient — the rate at which material lines spin without deforming. Pure shear has zero vorticity, simple shear has plenty, and the ratio of the two is what makes a deformation coaxial or non-coaxial.

Wavelength (fold)

The repeat distance of a fold train — the distance between adjacent hinges of the same sense. In buckling it is set by layer thickness and viscosity contrast, which is why it is such a useful diagnostic.

Wilson cycle

The life story of an ocean basin, and the single most useful frame for reading a continent’s history. A continent rifts; the rift floods and becomes a narrow sea; sea-floor spreading widens it into a mature ocean with quiet, subsiding passive margins on both sides; somewhere a subduction zone starts and the ocean begins to close; the last of the ocean floor is consumed and the two continental margins collide, welding into one landmass with a mountain belt along the suture. Then the whole thing can begin again — often along the same old weakness. Named for J. Tuzo Wilson, who argued that the Atlantic had closed and reopened close to the same line. The point to carry away is that ocean floor is temporary and continents are not: the same fragments are rifted and re-assembled over and over, which is why ancient sutures keep being reactivated long after the ocean that made them has gone.

World Stress Map

The global compilation of contemporary crustal stress orientations in the upper 40 km, built from focal mechanisms, borehole breakouts, in-situ measurements and neotectonic structures, and colour-coded by tectonic regime. It shows a broad correlation between maximum horizontal stress and plate motion — plus enough exceptions to prove that many sources of stress act at once. Note that it gives orientation, not magnitude.

Yield stress

The stress threshold above which permanent, plastic deformation begins. Below it a material is effectively elastic; at it the material deforms freely and the stress is capped — no matter how hard you push, it cannot carry more. Temperature, fluids and lower confining pressure all reduce it.

Young’s modulus (EEE)

The elastic stiffness in simple stretching. Typical crustal rocks are around 40–100 GPa. Note that stiffness is not strength: EEE tells you how much a rock deflects under load, not how much load it can take before breaking.
