## Fault Terminology
<div class="cols">
<div class="wide">

Hanging wall – rock body above fault plane (wall)
Footwall – rock body below a fault plane
Net slip vector – connects two formerly adjacent points (strike-slip and dip-slip components)
Rake (pitch) – the angle between the strike of the slip surface and the slip vector
Throw – apparent vertical displacement; or the vertical component of dip separation.
Heave – apparent horizontal displacement; or the horizontal component of dip separation.
Ramps & flats: alternating steep and subhorizontal portions of any fault surface.

van der Pluijm & Marshall (2003)

</div>

## Three Major Types of Faults
<div class="cols">
<div class="wide">

- Normal Faults: the hanging wall is lowered or downthrown relative to the footwall.
- Reverse Faults: the hanging wall is upthrown relative to the footwall.
- Strike-slip faults: movement is lateral in the horizontal plane (sinistral: left-lateral; dextral: right-lateral).

<div class="cols">
<div>

![](Module-iv-Brittle-Deformation/Lecture6-extracted/slide014_img1.jpg)

<p class="caption">Fossen, 2011</p>

</div>

- The interaction of structure (e.g., orientation) and topography (including expose) and by the absence of minor structures complicates the interpretation of fault slip
- Separation is the offset observed along faults in 2D views of outcrops, or geologic cross-sections. It refers only to the apparent sense and magnitude of offset along faults.

Note: fault trace = the intersection line of the fault plane and the section line/ surface

Slip is fully determined when we know for individual faults, and for the whole faulted body, the magnitude of displacement, the direction of dis- placement, and the sense of displacement. 

Difficult of find the movement in practical case: due to erosion, different angle of cross section, structural burial

## Fault Zone
<div class="cols">
<div class="wide">

- Fault core is the central slip surface of a fault zone, and it separates from the brittlely deformed wallrock known as fault damage zone.
- The damage zone shows a density of brittle deformation higher than the background levels. Structures in this zone include deformation bands, shear fractures, tensile fractures and stylolites.

## Fault Zone Structures
<div class="cols">
<div class="wide">

- A general positive correlation between fault displacement and the thickness of both damage zone and fault core.
- A fault core has several major characteristics:
- Brittle fault rocks: when fault movements alter the original rock sufficiently it is turned into a brittle fault rock, including fault breccia, gouge, pseudotachylyte and cataclasite.
- Slickenslides and slip lineations: good indicator of shear sense
- Fault-related folding → drag

</div>
<div>

![](Module-iv-Brittle-Deformation/Lecture6-extracted/slide018_img1.jpg)

<p class="caption">To be improved · Fossen, 2011</p>

</div>
</div>

Note:
The ratio between fault displacement D and damage zone thickness DT is the same for any fault size, and the distance between adjacent lines in this figure represents one order of magnitude. Much of the data in Figure 8.12a plot around or above the line D=DT, meaning that the fault displacement is close to or some- what larger than the damage zone thickness, at least for faults with displacements up to 100 meters. 

A similar relationship exists between fault core thick- ness (CT) and fault displacement. This relationship is constrained by the straight lines D = 1000CT and D =10CT, meaning that the fault core is statistically around 1/100 of the fault displacement for faults with displacements up to 100 meters.

## Pseudotachylyte
<div class="cols">
<div>

![](Module-iv-Brittle-Deformation/Lecture6-extracted/slide022_img1.jpg)

<p class="caption">Mylonitic gneiss · Pseudotachylyte · Pseudotachylyte injection veins · Fossen, 2011</p>

<div class="wide">

- Pseudo-, means “like”; tachylyte is a type of volcanic glass. But pseudotachylyte has nothing to do with volcanos.
- Its formation requires a powerful shock under very high-pressure conditions and an instantaneously imposed strain. The frictional heating of faulting causes the already hot rocks to melt and occurs typically deeper than 4 km.
- Pseudotachylite occurrences are in essence fossil records of deep earthquakes (Sibson, 1980).

The slickenlines are little gouged grooves where asperities (bumps) on the block of rock opposite this one ground into this rock along the fault surface as the two moved relative to one another. So did our block of granite move to the left or the right relative to the missing block? Let’s zoom in to find out. Little “steps” on the surface of the fault face to the left. If you were to run your finger over this outcrop from right to left, it would feel pretty smooth. On the other hand, running your finger over it from left to right, your fingertip would catch on those steps (and smaller, similarly-oriented steps), and the passage would feel rough and halting, if not downright painful.

## Tangent-lineation Diagrams
<div class="cols">
<div class="wide">

- This diagram provides a geometric way of extracting stress from fault slip data.
- Plotting each fault plane and its lineation, the M-plane, which contains the pole to the fault plane and the lineation, and by drawing an arrow tangent to the M-plane at this pole. The direction of the arrow reflects the movement of the footwall relative to the hanging wall.
- Combining many such measurements in a fault zone, and the resulting pattern may reveal both the orientations and the relative magnitudes of the principal stress axes.

Slip plane

</div>
<div>

![](Module-iv-Brittle-Deformation/Lecture6-extracted/slide025_img1.jpg) <!-- .element style="width:72%;" -->

<p class="caption">Fossen, 2011</p>

</div>
</div>

Note:
Schematic illustration of pitch as measured on a fault surface. (b) The pitch plotted in a stereonet (lower hemisphere, equal area). This projection also shows how the tangent-lineation is found for a known slip plane (as shown in (a)) and its lineation (striation) by plotting a plane (the movement or M-plane) that contains the lineation and the pole to the slip plane. The tangent-lineation (red arrow) is the arrow drawn tangential to the M-plane at the pole to the slip plane. Its direction describes the movement of the footwall relative to the hanging wall; in the present case a normal movement, where the footwall moves up to the west.

<div class="wide">

- Before the tips have reached each other (but after their strain fields have started to interfere) the faults are said to underlap.
- Once the tips have passed each other, the faults are overlapping. Under- and overlapping faults are said to be soft linked as long as they are not in direct physical contact. The increase of local displacement gradient results in the ramp-like folds.
- Eventually the faults may link up to form a hard link.

</div>
</div>

Note:
Underlapping faults “feel” the presence of a neighboring fault tip in the sense that the energy required to keep the deformation going increases. The propagation rate of the fault tips in the area of underlap is thus reduced, which causes the local displacement gradient to increase. This makes the overlap zone folded. the folding is well expressed in the form of a ramp-like fold.
